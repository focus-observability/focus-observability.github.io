What Your Policy Cannot See: Robot Data Curation via Observability Estimation

Anonymous authors

Paper under double-blind review

Abstract Method Experiments Results

TL;DR: FOCUS estimates whether robot demonstrations contain enough visual information to infer the demonstrated actions, then uses these estimates to curate data for imitation learning.

Abstract

Robot imitation learning performance depends critically on the quality of demonstration data. Even successful demonstrations may omit action-relevant visual information, an aspect of data quality that remains less explored in robot data curation. We introduce FOCUS, an information-theoretic framework for quantifying visual observability in offline robot demonstrations, defined as the extent to which visual observations contain the action-relevant information needed to determine an appropriate action. Our key idea is to test whether an auxiliary observation provides information about the demonstration action beyond what is contained in the visual observation. We instantiate this idea using conditional mutual information between the auxiliary observation and actions given visual observations. We use the resulting observability scores to rank demonstrations, filter those with low visual observability, and train imitation learning policies on the retained data. Empirically, our scores generally rank demonstrations in agreement with human observability judgments across two simulated and four real-robot manipulation tasks. The best-performing FOCUS configurations improve success rates by 5–45 percentage points over training on the full dataset.

Method

FOCUS compares two conditional action distributions: one conditioned on the policy observation, and another that also receives an auxiliary observation. If the auxiliary observation provides additional information about the action, the policy observation is missing action-relevant information.

Sobs = −I(A; Oaux | O)

Higher scores indicate greater observability. FOCUS-extra uses an additional exterior camera as the auxiliary observation. FOCUS-robot uses robot proprioception. We estimate scores for individual transitions and aggregate the lowest-scoring fraction within each trajectory, emphasizing the least observable moments. Demonstrations are then ranked by their trajectory scores, and a selected fraction is retained for policy training.

Experiments

We evaluate FOCUS on two simulated tasks, Thread Needle and Insert Fork, and four real-robot tasks, Place Pen in Cup, Insert Straw into Cup, Hang Cup, and Fit Tool into Holder. All datasets contain successful demonstrations with varying visibility during task-critical phases.

Policy Learning Results

We compare FOCUS with training on all demonstrations, random selection at matched retention ratios, DemInf, and QoQ. Policies are evaluated with 100 rollouts on Thread Needle, 200 on Insert Fork, and 20 on each real-robot task.

On Place Pen in Cup, FOCUS-extra achieves 80% success using 40% of demonstrations. On Fit Tool into Holder, FOCUS-robot achieves 85% success at the same retention ratio. The matched-size random subsets show that performance depends on which demonstrations are retained.

Limitations. The gains depend on the task and curation configuration. Filtering can remove coverage and diversity needed for policy learning, so retaining fewer demonstrations does not always improve performance.

Observability Analysis

Observability varies across the phases of a demonstration. Aggregating the lowest-scoring transitions can reveal missing information during task-critical interactions; the choice of aggregation fraction also affects how well scores distinguish fully and partially observable trajectories.
